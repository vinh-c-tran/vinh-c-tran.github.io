Van der Waals Injection Molding

Growing shaped, ultraflat metal crystals inside hBN-lined molds.

Summary

My colleagues and I developed a novel thin film growth technique that grows single crystals hallbars and nanowires inbetween layers of hexagonal Boron Nitride (hBN), a Van der Waals material, via pressure assisted melt growth atop a mold.

The work achieves growth of nanostructures such as nanowires without the need for post-growth fabrication such as reactive ion etching and the growth occurs in between layers of hBN, protecting the samples from oxidation and environmental contaminants as well as imprinting an atomically smooth surface onto the crystals. Metrology and electronic transport measurements then

My involvement in this project was to develop this technique, refine process parameters once proof of concept, and then test and verify through metrology and electrical characterization. This culminated in a first author paper Van der Waals injection-molded crystals, published in npj 2D Materials and Applications 9, 109 (2025)

injection-molded bismuth, tin, and indium crystals in geometries including Hall bars, rings, rectangles, and nanowires. For bismuth, changing the trench depth produces crystal thicknesses from 11 to 106 nm. Diffraction measurements and low-temperature electrical transport connect that geometric control to crystal quality and reduced disorder scattering.

General approach

Crystalline thin film metals are grown directly in between layers of hexagonal boron nitride (hBN), a van der Waals material, atop an etched SiO2/Si mold substrate by a pressure assisted melt growth process. Bulk source material is encapsulated in between hBN layers and transferred onto an etched substrate. The strcture is heated to the melting point and pressurized, forcing the molten material flow and conform to the mold. The hBN provides both an atomically smooth molding surface and hermetic sealing during and after this process while the SiO2/Si mold provides geometry control.

The mold separates two kinds of control: an etched SiO₂ trench sets the lateral shape and available depth, while the hBN surfaces define the crystal interfaces. A source flake sits between bottom and top hBN layers over the trench. Heating melts the source; compression pushes it into the mold; cooling under maintained pressure crystallizes the material in place.

The molten material moves away from the starting flake’s oxide shell, which largely stays near its original location. Encapsulation then limits further oxidation. This is a way to shape a crystal during growth rather than relying on post-growth etching of its channel.

Sample growth: Application of Nanofabrication Skills

  1. Create SiO2 Mold - Electron Beam Lithography and Reactive Ion Etching/Wet Etching Our molds consist of etched trenches in an 285 nm oxide SiO2/Si substrate.
    1. CAD Pattern Design. The first step is to create the pattern of the geometry an a CAD program (AutoCad or DesignCad). Depending on the resolution of the features, this ends up being a multilayer design with careful and precise positioning of features.
    2. Electron Beam Lithography. I then write this pattern onto SiO2/Si substrates spun with a layer of PMMA, it is our positive resist used in this process, using electron beam lithography implemented in scanning electron microscope (SEM) (FEI Magellan).
    3. EtchingAfter developing, this results in a PMMA mask which defines the geometry. Using EBL, we have made nanowire molds down to ~100 nm wide.
    4. With this mask, we then etch a trench to our desired depth using either reactive ion etching (CF4) or wet etching (flourine based etchant).
  2. Assemble the hBN stack. Use PC/PDMS transfer to place bottom hBN, the source flake, and top hBN. The hBN layers conform to the trench; the reported process uses selected hBN flakes 5–60 nm thick.
  3. Melt and fill. Apply compression through a flat top substrate while heating the source. The paper estimates applied pressures of 10–100 MPa. Maintain pressure during cooling.
  4. Transfer for measurement. Remove the encapsulated crystal from the mold using polymer-assisted transfer, then place it on a substrate for AFM, diffraction, or electrical-device fabrication.

Geometry and thickness

The study demonstrates planar features ranging from 150 nm to 30 μm in width and up to 70 μm in length. For bismuth, crystal thickness increases approximately linearly with trench depth. Using a sapphire top pressing substrate improves thickness uniformity compared with the more compliant glass substrate; the reported sapphire-molded samples include thickness variations of 1.7–3.6%.

Growth is material-dependent. Tin and indium can show faceted edges and internal voids that evolve with time or heating. These observations matter when transferring the approach to a new material or device geometry.

Structural and crystal characterization

AFM: thickness, flatness, and roughness

AFM measures thickness relative to nearby hBN/hBN regions after transfer out of the trench. Measurements across the channel show mesa-like profiles, while removal of the top hBN exposes terraces several micrometers across. Terrace-scale roughness is around one-tenth of a nanometer in the selected tin and bismuth regions below.

Diffraction: crystal domains and orientation

Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) diffraction probe crystal orientation and domain structure. Seven of the eight examined bismuth crystals were single crystals; one contained a domain wall separating two in-plane orientations. Both (111) and (110) out-of-plane orientations were observed, with four samples of each orientation.

For the (110) sample S58, AFM terrace steps average 0.33 ± 0.05 nm, consistent with the expected 0.328 nm bilayer spacing. The combination of topography and diffraction is important: surface flatness alone does not establish crystallinity.

Electrical characterization

Contact fabrication and measurement

After transfer to an unetched SiO₂/Si chip, electron-beam lithography defines the contact regions. SF₆ reactive-ion etching opens the top hBN, followed by ion milling to clean the exposed bismuth and metal deposition to form contacts. The transport measurements use low-frequency lock-in techniques in a closed-cycle cryostat with a field range up to 12 T. The contact-fabrication sequence is documented in Supplementary Fig. S7.

S51: main-text transport results

For the 106 nm device S51, the paper reports a residual resistance ratio of approximately 4 and a residual resistivity of 3.2 × 10−5 Ω·cm at 4 K. The magnetoresistance reaches 3686% at 2.5 T, and resistance oscillations become visible near 2 T. Together, these measurements support the interpretation of reduced disorder scattering in the molded crystal.

S56: supplementary transport results

The second device, S56, is the subject of Supplementary Fig. S8. Its caption describes an approximately 40 nm crystal; the SI height-analysis table gives a mean thickness of 42.01 nm. The measured resistance has a nonmonotonic temperature dependence, with a residual resistance ratio of 1.5 and a reported resistivity of 6.94 × 10−5 Ω·cm at 1.5 K.

The field sweep extends to 12 T and shows a large positive magnetoresistance. Subtracting a smooth background reveals resistance oscillations plotted against inverse magnetic field. These measurements extend the transport characterization to a thinner molded crystal.

Measurement geometry. The SI specifies a 3.7 μm voltage-probe separation for S56’s temperature sweep. Its field sweep uses a 7.5 μm channel width and a 16.8 μm voltage-probe separation. The two panels therefore should not be treated as the same resistance measurement geometry.
